E1 · Descriptive Statistics

11 practice problems on types of data, sampling methods, measures of central tendency and variation. Problems E1.1 to E1.11.

Key Formulas · Descriptive Statistics
Direct mean: \(\bar{x} = \dfrac{\sum x}{n}\)
Step-deviation mean: \(\bar{x} = A + \dfrac{\sum d_i}{n},\quad d_i = x_i - A\)
Sample variance: \(s^2 = \dfrac{\sum x^2 - (\sum x)^2/n}{n-1}\)
Standard deviation: \(s = \sqrt{s^2}\)
Coefficient of Variation: \(\text{CV} = \dfrac{s}{\bar{x}} \times 100\%\)
Harmonic Mean: \(\text{HM} = \dfrac{n}{\sum(1/x_i)}\)   (use for equal-distance speeds)
Geometric Mean (growth rates): \(\text{GM} = \left[(1+r_1)(1+r_2)\cdots(1+r_n)\right]^{1/n} - 1\)   (use for compounding returns; handles negative rates)
Symmetric distribution: Mean = Median = Mode
Right skewed: Mode < Median < Mean
Left skewed: Mean < Median < Mode
Decision rule: Smaller CV = more consistent = better instrument / process
E1.1 Types of data according to the collection procedure

Explain the types of data according to the types of collection procedure, using a practical example for each type.

Solution

According to how data are collected, data fall into two types.

1. Primary data · collected by the investigator directly from the source, for the first time, for the specific purpose of the present enquiry. Collection is costly but the data are more accurate and relevant.

EEE example: a student measures output voltage of a multimeter at five current settings in his own Ohm's law lab · he generates those readings himself, so they are primary data. Other EEE examples: strain gauge readings from a materials lab, current and voltage data logged from a circuit under test.
CSE example: a programmer runs a sorting algorithm on 20 different input files and records the execution time for each run directly from their own machine · those timing measurements are primary data. Other CSE examples: response times measured by running a custom benchmark on a new CPU, or user-survey data collected by the student themselves.

2. Secondary data · data collected by somebody else, for some other purpose, and reused by the present investigator. Cheap and immediately available, but the investigator cannot control the definitions or quality.

EEE example: the 1999 city population figures taken from a world population database to study ozone concentration; annual road accident records published by BRTA; a manufacturer's failure rate tables for a device.
CSE example: benchmark results downloaded from the SPEC CPU suite published by a third party; a public dataset from Kaggle used to train a machine learning model; GitHub repository statistics pulled via API · all collected by someone else for a different purpose.

Key point: the same figure can be primary for the original collector and secondary for any later user.
E1.2 Quota sampling

Discuss the method of quota sampling with a practical example.

Solution

Quota sampling is a non probability sampling method. The population is divided into mutually exclusive groups. A fixed quota · usually proportional to each group's size · is decided in advance. The interviewer then fills each quota by selecting whichever units are convenient; there is no random selection at the last stage.

Steps: (i) Choose control characteristics and divide the population. (ii) Fix the quota for each group. (iii) Field workers select units until each quota is complete.

Practical example. A company wants the opinion of 200 AUST students on a new mobile app. The student body is 60% engineering, 25% business, 15% others, so the quotas are 120, 50 and 30. The interviewer stands at the campus gate and interviews any 120 engineering students, any 50 business students and any 30 others who pass by.

Merits: fast, cheap, no sampling frame needed, guarantees group representation. Demerits: not random at the last stage, so interviewer bias enters; no valid confidence interval can be attached to the result.

E1.3 Shape of a distribution and relative positions of mean, median and mode

Discuss the shape of the distribution of any assumed population data regarding the relative positions of the arithmetic mean, median and mode.

Solution

The three averages sit in a fixed order that reveals the shape of the distribution.

(i) Symmetric: \(\text{Mean} = \text{Median} = \text{Mode}\). Example: resistance values from a well controlled machine.

(ii) Positively skewed (right skewed): \(\text{Mode} < \text{Median} < \text{Mean}\). A few large values pull the mean right. Example: repair times, incomes, waiting times.

(iii) Negatively skewed (left skewed): \(\text{Mean} < \text{Median} < \text{Mode}\). A few very small values pull the mean left. Example: lifetime of a well designed component.

Empirical rule: \(\text{Mean} - \text{Mode} \approx 3(\text{Mean} - \text{Median})\). This is the basis of Pearson's coefficient of skewness \(S_k = (\bar{x} - \text{Mode})/s\).

E1.4 How measures of variation help in engineering

Explain, with a practical example, how measures of variation help us in the field of engineering, and in particular how they help us select the better of two instruments in the lab.

Solution

An average alone hides the risk. Two instruments can have the same mean and still behave completely differently · it is the variation that decides which is acceptable.

Why variation matters: (1) Precision: a smaller \(s\) means readings cluster close to the mean. (2) Quality control: control limits are built from \(\sigma\), not \(\bar{x}\). (3) Unit free comparison: \(\text{CV} = (s/\bar{x})\times100\%\) compares instruments on different scales. (4) Risk: in reliability, the spread of the lifetime distribution decides the warranty period.

EEE example. Two multimeters checked at the same input: Meter 1 \(\bar{x}=2.975\) V, \(s=0.25\) V, CV = 8.40%; Meter 2 \(\bar{x}=2.925\) V, \(s=0.15\) V, CV = 5.13%. Both read almost the same average, but Meter 2 scatters only about half as much. Without the measure of variation, the two meters would look identical.

CSE example. Two sorting algorithms are timed on the same 10 input files. Algorithm A: \(\bar{x}=120\) ms, \(s=18\) ms, CV = 15%. Algorithm B: \(\bar{x}=130\) ms, \(s=10.4\) ms, CV = 8%. Algorithm A is faster on average, but Algorithm B is more consistent. For a real-time system where worst-case performance matters, Algorithm B is preferable · a judgment that is impossible without the measure of variation.

Meter 2 is the better instrument (lower CV = more consistent).
E1.5 Mean processing time of a CPU by two different methods

A computer hardware company measures the CPU processing time (in milliseconds) for executing a set of tasks under heavy load. The data are:

32, 45, 38, 40, 47, 42, 36, 50, 43, 39

Calculate the mean processing time by an appropriate method. Also use another method, different from the first, and compare the two results.

Solution

Method 1 · Direct method.

\(\displaystyle\bar{x} = \frac{\sum x}{n} = \frac{32+45+38+40+47+42+36+50+43+39}{10} = \frac{412}{10} = 41.2 \text{ ms}\)

Method 2 · Step deviation method. Take assumed mean \(A = 40\), \(d_i = x_i - A\):

\(x_i\)32453840474236504339
\(d_i\)−85−2072−4103−1

\(\sum d_i = 12\), so \(\bar{x} = 40 + 12/10 = 41.2\text{ ms}\)

Both methods give exactly 41.2 ms. The step deviation method is algebraically identical; its advantage is that the deviations are smaller numbers, reducing arithmetic errors.

Mean CPU processing time = 41.2 ms (both methods agree)
E1.6 Which server configuration is more consistent?

Configuration A: mean response time 125 ms, standard deviation 10 ms. Configuration B: mean response time 130 ms, standard deviation 12 ms.

(i) Calculate the coefficient of variation for both.   (ii) Decide which is more consistent and justify.

Solution

Since the means differ, use the coefficient of variation: \(\text{CV} = \dfrac{s}{\bar{x}}\times 100\%\)

(i) \(\text{CV}_A = \dfrac{10}{125}\times100 = 8.00\%\)    \(\text{CV}_B = \dfrac{12}{130}\times100 = 9.23\%\)

(ii) Consistency means small relative variation. Since \(\text{CV}_A = 8.00\% < \text{CV}_B = 9.23\%\), Configuration A is more consistent. Configuration A also has the smaller mean (125 ms vs 130 ms), so it is better on both counts.

Configuration A is more consistent: CV = 8.00% vs 9.23%.
E1.7 Choosing the better multimeter · four readings each

An Ohm's law experiment is run with two multimeters, each at the same input current levels. Apply the proper statistical method to choose the better multimeter.

MultimeterOutput Voltage (V)
Meter 12.73.02.93.3
Meter 22.83.12.83.0
Solution · Coefficient of Variation

The two meters have different means, so use CV for a unit free comparison.

Meter 1: \(n=4\), \(\sum x=11.9\), \(\bar{x}_1=2.975\) V, \(\sum x^2=35.59\)

\(s_1=\sqrt{(35.59-11.9^2/4)/3}=\sqrt{0.1875/3}=0.25\text{ V}\), \(\;\text{CV}_1=0.25/2.975\times100=8.40\%\)

Meter 2: \(n=4\), \(\sum x=11.7\), \(\bar{x}_2=2.925\) V, \(\sum x^2=34.29\)

\(s_2=\sqrt{(34.29-11.7^2/4)/3}=\sqrt{0.0675/3}=0.15\text{ V}\), \(\;\text{CV}_2=0.15/2.925\times100=5.13\%\)

CV₂ = 5.13% < CV₁ = 8.40% → Multimeter 2 is the better (more consistent) instrument.
E1.8 Choosing the better multimeter · five readings each

Same experiment as E1.7, with one additional reading from each meter:

MultimeterOutput Voltage (V)
Meter 12.73.02.93.33.5
Meter 22.83.12.83.03.2
Solution

Meter 1: \(\sum x=15.4\), \(\bar{x}_1=3.08\), \(\sum x^2=47.84\)

\(s_1=\sqrt{(47.84-15.4^2/5)/4}=\sqrt{0.408/4}=0.3194\text{ V}\), \(\;\text{CV}_1=10.37\%\)

\(s_2=\sqrt{(44.53-14.9^2/5)/4}=\sqrt{0.128/4}=0.1789\text{ V}\), \(\;\text{CV}_2=6.00\%\)

Adding the fifth reading increased both CVs slightly but did not change the ranking.

E1.9 Comparing two multimeters over a wide range of input currents

The output voltages at the same input currents are:

I (amp)0.50.7135
Meter 1 (V)2.53.591523
Meter 2 (V)2.43.291522

Apply a suitable statistical method to decide which meter is better.

Solution

Meter 1: \(\sum x=53\), \(\bar{x}_1=10.6\), \(\sum x^2=853.5\)

\(s_1=\sqrt{(853.5-53^2/5)/4}=\sqrt{291.7/4}=8.540\text{ V}\), \(\;\text{CV}_1=80.56\%\)

E1.10 Percentage variation of concrete density

The recorded densities (kg/m³) of a type of concrete under varying moisture conditions are:

2400, 2425, 2430, 2410, 2385

Find the percentage of variation of the densities and discuss the finding.

Solution

\(\sum x=12050\), \(n=5\), \(\bar{x}=12050/5=2410\text{ kg/m}^3\)

\(\sum x^2=29{,}041{,}850\), \(\quad \sum x^2 - (\sum x)^2/n = 29{,}041{,}850 - 12050^2/5 = 1350\)

\(s=\sqrt{1350/4}=\sqrt{337.5}=18.37\text{ kg/m}^3\)

\(\text{CV}=18.37/2410\times100=0.76\%\)

Discussion: A CV below 1% is extremely small. The density is almost unaffected by moisture variation · readings deviate from the mean by less than 1%. For a construction material this is very desirable: the mix is homogeneous, and design values based on 2410 kg/m³ can be used with confidence.

CV = 0.76% · extremely low; concrete density is very consistent.
E1.11 Average speed over four routes of equal distance

You rode a bike at 55.5, 83, 77, and 89.5 km/hr through four routes of equal distance. Choose the best average, give the reason, and find the result.

Solution

Choice: Harmonic Mean. Speed is distance/time. When a rate is averaged over a constant numerator (equal distances), the harmonic mean gives the correct result · total distance / total time. The arithmetic mean is correct only for equal times, which is not the case here.

\(\dfrac{1}{55.5}=0.018018,\quad \dfrac{1}{83}=0.012048,\quad \dfrac{1}{77}=0.012987,\quad \dfrac{1}{89.5}=0.011173\)

\(\sum(1/x_i)=0.054226\)

\(\text{HM}=\dfrac{n}{\sum(1/x_i)}=\dfrac{4}{0.054226}=73.76\text{ km/hr}\)

The arithmetic mean (76.25 km/hr) overstates the true average speed by about 2.5 km/hr.

Average speed = 73.76 km/hr (Harmonic Mean)
E1.12 Mean rate of return · SHELL group investment

SHELL group had an investment which produced returns of 22, 30, −15 and 8 percent in four successive years. Choose the best option for finding the mean rate of return, give the reason for your choice, and then find the mean return.

Solution

Choice: Geometric Mean. Rates of return compound multiplicatively · each year's ending value equals the starting value multiplied by \((1+r)\). The arithmetic mean ignores compounding and overstates the true average growth. The harmonic mean applies when a constant numerator is divided by varying rates (e.g. equal distances at different speeds) · not the situation here. Since one return is negative (\(-15\%\)), the standard GM formula (direct product of raw percentages) cannot be used; we apply the modified form with \((1+r)\) factors instead.

Formula: \[\text{GM} = \left[(1+r_1)(1+r_2)(1+r_3)(1+r_4)\right]^{1/4} - 1\]

Calculation:

\[(1.22)\times(1.30)\times(0.85)\times(1.08) = 1.4559\]

\[\text{GM} = (1.4559)^{1/4} - 1 = 1.0985 - 1 = 0.0985\]

Mean rate of return = 9.85% (Geometric Mean)

E1.15 Stratified random sampling · method and practical example

Discuss the stratified random sampling method with a practical example.

Solution

Definition. The population is first divided into non-overlapping, homogeneous subgroups called strata. A random sample (simple or systematic) is then drawn independently from every stratum. Observations within a stratum are similar to each other; observations across strata are different.

Procedure. (1) Identify a stratifying variable (department, gender, region, grade). (2) List every member of each stratum as a sampling frame. (3) Decide the sample size per stratum · proportional allocation: \(n_i = n \times N_i/N\). (4) Draw a random sample of size \(n_i\) from stratum \(i\). (5) Combine all sub-samples.

EEE example. A quality engineer surveys 100 out of 500 electronic components from three production shifts (Morning: 200, Afternoon: 200, Night: 100). Proportional allocation: Morning 40, Afternoon 40, Night 20. Random samples are drawn from each shift's output independently, ensuring all shifts are represented.

CSE example. A software QA team tests 60 modules from a codebase split into three layers (UI: 150 modules, Backend: 250, Database: 100). Proportional allocation: UI 18, Backend 30, Database 12. Random samples from each layer guarantee all architectural tiers are covered.

Advantage: guarantees representation from every stratum; reduces sampling error compared to simple random sampling when strata differ meaningfully.

Stratified sampling = divide into homogeneous strata, random sample from each. Guarantees representation of all subgroups.
E1.16 Cluster sampling · method, example, and difference from stratified

Explain the method of cluster sampling with an example, and state its key difference from stratified random sampling.

Solution

Definition. The population is divided into naturally occurring groups called clusters (e.g. city blocks, school classes, factory batches). A random sample of clusters is selected; then all members of the chosen clusters are surveyed.

Procedure. (1) Identify natural groupings (clusters). (2) Randomly select \(k\) clusters. (3) Collect data from every unit in those \(k\) clusters.

EEE example. An engineer must inspect circuit boards produced in 50 daily batches. Randomly selecting 8 batches and inspecting every board in those 8 batches is cluster sampling · far cheaper than inspecting selected boards from every batch.

CSE example. A researcher studying server logs selects 5 data centers at random from 30 and downloads all logs from those 5 centers. Each data center is a cluster containing all the servers and logs for that site.

Key difference from stratified sampling:

FeatureStratifiedCluster
Internal compositionHomogeneous within strataHeterogeneous within clusters
Who is sampledSelected units from every stratumAll units from selected clusters only
PurposePrecision · represent all subgroupsCost efficiency · reduce travel/access cost
Missing groupsNo stratum is omittedUnselected clusters are entirely ignored
Cluster: randomly choose groups, survey everyone inside. Stratified: divide into groups, randomly pick from each. Strata = homogeneous; clusters = heterogeneous.
E1.17 Simple random sampling · method and difference from stratified

Discuss the method of simple random sampling with an example. Also explain the basic difference between stratified and simple random sampling.

Solution

Definition. Every member of the population has an equal and independent probability of being selected. No sub-grouping is made. The sample is drawn by lottery, random number tables, or a random number generator.

Procedure. (1) List every population member and assign a unique number. (2) Use a random number table or software to select \(n\) distinct numbers. (3) Include the corresponding members in the sample.

EEE example. A factory produces 800 resistors per day. An engineer numbers them 001·800 and uses a random number generator to pick 40 for quality testing · each resistor has the same 40/800 = 5% chance of selection.

CSE example. A database holds 5000 user accounts. A developer randomly selects 200 account IDs using a computer-generated list to test a new search feature · each account is equally likely to be chosen, so the sample is unbiased.

Difference between simple random and stratified sampling:

FeatureSimple Random SamplingStratified Sampling
GroupingNo prior groupingPopulation divided into strata first
RepresentationSubgroups may be under or over represented by chanceEvery stratum is guaranteed a fixed representation
PrecisionLower precision when population is heterogeneousHigher precision for heterogeneous populations
ComplexitySimple to executeRequires knowledge of the population structure
Best forHomogeneous populations or when no grouping is knownPopulations with clearly defined, distinct subgroups
SRS: every member equally likely; no structure assumed. Stratified: sub-populations defined first; each is sampled separately · better precision for heterogeneous populations.
