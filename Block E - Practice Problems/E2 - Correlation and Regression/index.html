E2 · Correlation and Regression

13 practice problems on scatter diagrams, Pearson’s r, simple linear regression, and the coefficient of determination. Problems E2.1 to E2.13.

Key Formulas · Correlation and Regression
Corrected sums: \(S_{xx}=\sum x^2-\dfrac{(\sum x)^2}{n},\qquad S_{yy}=\sum y^2-\dfrac{(\sum y)^2}{n},\qquad S_{xy}=\sum xy-\dfrac{(\sum x)(\sum y)}{n}\)
Slope: \(\hat{\beta}_1 = \dfrac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}\)
Intercept: \(\hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1\bar{x}\)
Fitted line: \(\hat{y} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x\)
Pearson's r: \(r = \dfrac{S_{xy}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}S_{yy}}},\quad -1 \le r \le 1\)
Coefficient of determination: \(R^2 = \dfrac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx}S_{yy}} = 1 - \dfrac{\text{ESS}}{\text{TSS}}\)
\(\hat{\beta}_1\): average change in \(y\) per one-unit increase in \(x\) (state the units)
\(R^2\): % of variation in \(y\) explained by \(x\); remainder is unexplained
Warning: never predict far outside the observed range of \(x\) (extrapolation)
E2.1 Uses of regression analysis in prediction

Explain, with a practical example, how regression analysis helps for prediction purposes in the field of science and engineering.

Solution

Regression analysis expresses the dependent variable \(y\) as a function of predictor(s) \(x\). Once the function is estimated from past data, \(y\) can be predicted for any new \(x\) without repeating the experiment.

What regression gives the engineer: (1) Prediction: estimate the response at a new setting, saving the cost of a physical test. (2) Quantifying the effect: the slope gives how much \(y\) changes per unit change in \(x\). (3) Control: inverting the line tells us what \(x\) to set to achieve a target \(y\). (4) Explanation: \(R^2\) tells how much variation in \(y\) the predictor accounts for.

EEE example. A research engineer fits: \(\widehat{\text{DC output}} = 0.2602 + 0.2180\times(\text{wind velocity})\). The slope says every extra mph of wind raises output by 0.218 V. Without testing further, the engineer predicts output at 12 mph as 2.876 V, and 88.95% of variability in the output is explained by wind velocity alone.

CSE example. A software engineer records the input size (MB) and execution time (seconds) of a data-processing script across 8 runs and fits: \(\widehat{\text{time}} = 0.5 + 0.3\times(\text{input size})\). The slope of 0.3 says every extra MB adds 0.3 seconds. Without running a new test, the engineer predicts execution time for any new input size and confirms that the algorithm scales linearly with data volume.

Caution: regression is a statistical association, not proof of causation, and is valid only within the range of \(x\) covered by the data.

E2.2 A practical use of Karl Pearson's correlation coefficient in electrical engineering

Discuss a practical use of Karl Pearson's correlation coefficient in the field of electrical engineering.

Solution

Karl Pearson's coefficient \(r = S_{xy}/\sqrt{S_{xx}S_{yy}}\), where \(-1\le r\le1\), is a unit free measure of the strength and direction of the linear relationship between two quantitative variables.

Practical uses in EEE: (1) Checking a physical law: before fitting DC output on wind velocity, computing \(r=0.943\) confirms a straight line is the right model. (2) Instrument agreement: correlation between a new sensor and a reference instrument measures how faithfully the sensor tracks the true value. (3) Screening predictors: with several candidate influences on a response, correlations show which to include in the model. (4) Signal processing: the correlation between a received waveform and a stored template is the basis of matched filter detection.

CSE use: (5) Code quality: before modelling defect count from cyclomatic complexity, computing \(r\) first confirms whether a linear relationship exists. A strong positive \(r\) (say \(r=0.87\)) tells the engineer that more complex modules generate more bugs and that a straight line model is appropriate.

EEE example. An engineer measures supply voltage and current drawn by a magnetic winding at five settings and obtains \(r=0.996\). The near perfect positive correlation confirms an almost exactly linear (ohmic) relation and justifies a straight line calibration.

CSE example. A developer measures the cyclomatic complexity and the number of reported bugs for 12 software modules and obtains \(r=0.87\). The strong positive correlation confirms that complexity is a good linear predictor of defect count, justifying a regression model for defect forecasting.

Limitations: \(r\) measures only linear association; it is sensitive to outliers; a high \(r\) does not establish causation.

E2.3 Coefficient of determination and the ozone–population regression

Discuss the coefficient of determination with an example. Data on 1999 populations (millions) and ozone concentration (ppb/hour) for five Bangladesh cities:

Population (millions)4.90.52.30.22.7
Ozone (ppb/hour)135128129124133

Estimate the regression model, explain the parameters, and predict ozone for a city with population 141 million.

Solution

\(R^2 = 1 - \text{RSS}/T\!SS\) is the fraction of total variation in \(y\) explained by the regression on \(x\).

\(n=5\); \(\sum x=10.6\), \(\sum y=649\), \(\sum xy=1406.1\), \(\sum x^2=36.88\), \(\bar{x}=2.12\), \(\bar{y}=129.8\)

\(S_{xx}=36.88-10.6^2/5=14.408,\quad S_{xy}=1406.1-10.6\times649/5=30.22,\quad S_{yy}=74.8\)

\(\hat{\beta}_1=30.22/14.408=2.097,\quad \hat{\beta}_0=129.8-2.097(2.12)=125.35\)

\(\hat{y}=125.35+2.097\,x\)

Parameters: \(\hat{\beta}_1=2.097\): each additional million of population raises ozone by ~2.10 ppb/hr. \(\hat{\beta}_0=125.35\): background ozone level near zero population.

\(R^2=30.22^2/(14.408\times74.8)=84.74\%\)

At \(x=141\) million: \(\hat{y}=125.35+2.097(141)=421\) ppb/hr. Warning: the data range from 0.2 to 4.9 million · \(x=141\) is an extreme extrapolation with no statistical validity.

ŷ = 125.35 + 2.097x, R² = 84.74%. Prediction at x = 141 is extrapolation · not reliable.
E2.4 Windmill · DC output against wind velocity

Data on wind velocity and DC output of a windmill:

Wind velocity (mph)563.42.7109.7
DC output (V)1.5821.8221.0570.5002.2362.386

Estimate the regression, explain the parameters, find \(R^2\), and predict output at 12 mph.

Solution

\(n=6\), \(\sum x=36.8\), \(\sum y=9.583\), \(\sum xy=67.878\), \(\sum x^2=271.78\), \(\bar{x}=6.133\), \(\bar{y}=1.597\)

\(S_{xx}=271.78-36.8^2/6=46.007,\quad S_{xy}=67.878-36.8\times9.583/6=10.028,\quad S_{yy}\approx2.927\)

\(\hat{\beta}_1=10.028/46.007=0.2179,\quad \hat{\beta}_0=1.597-0.2179(6.133)=0.260\)

\(\hat{y}=0.260+0.218\,x\)

Parameters: slope 0.218: each extra mph of wind raises DC output by 0.218 V. Intercept 0.260: theoretical output at zero wind (near the data range · physically interpretable).

\(R^2=10.028^2/(46.007\times2.927)=88.95\%\)

At \(x=12\) mph: \(\hat{y}=0.260+0.218(12)=2.876\) V

ŷ = 0.260 + 0.218x, R² = 88.95%. Predicted output at 12 mph = 2.876 V.
E2.5 Website load time vs number of visitors

Data on page load time (s) versus number of visitors (000s) for a website:

Visitors (000s)205080110140180
Load time (s)0.751.552.313.143.884.91

Estimate the regression line of load time on visitors, find \(R^2\), and predict load time for 180,000 visitors.

Solution

\(n=6\), \(\sum x=580\), \(\sum y=16.54\), \(\sum xy=2022.3\), \(\sum x^2=72100\), \(\bar{x}=96.67\), \(\bar{y}=2.757\)

\(S_{xx}=72100-580^2/6=16006.7,\quad S_{xy}=2022.3-580\times16.54/6=413.87\)

\(\hat{\beta}_1=413.87/16006.7=0.02586,\quad \hat{\beta}_0=2.757-0.02586(96.67)=0.257\)

\(\hat{y}=0.257+0.0259\,x\)

\(R^2\approx99.89\%\) · an almost perfect linear fit.

At \(x=180\) (000s): \(\hat{y}=0.257+0.0259(180)=4.92\) s (within the data range).

ŷ = 0.257 + 0.0259x, R² = 99.89%. Load time at 180k visitors ≈ 4.91 s.

E2.7 Program efficiency vs data size · five observations

Data on program efficiency (%) versus data size (GB) for five runs:

Data size (GB)246810
Efficiency (%)8272645140

Estimate the regression line and \(R^2\).

Solution

\(n=5\), \(\sum x=30\), \(\sum y=309\), \(\sum xy=1672\), \(\sum x^2=220\), \(\bar{x}=6\), \(\bar{y}=61.8\)

\(S_{xx}=220-30^2/5=40,\quad S_{xy}=1672-30\times309/5=-182,\quad S_{yy}=\sum y^2-309^2/5=1088.8\)

\(\hat{\beta}_1=-182/40=-4.55,\quad \hat{\beta}_0=61.8-(-4.55)(6)=89.1\)

\(\hat{y}=89.1-4.55\,x\)

\(R^2=(-182)^2/(40\times1088.8)=33124/43552=76.1\%\)

Negative slope: efficiency decreases as data size increases. R² ≈ 76%.
E2.8 Program efficiency · six observations with extrapolation warning

Data on program efficiency (%) versus data size (GB) for six runs:

Data size (GB)24681012
Efficiency (%)827264514028

Estimate the regression line and predict efficiency at 16 GB.

Solution

\(n=6\), \(\sum x=42\), \(\sum y=337\), \(\sum xy=2038\), \(\sum x^2=364\), \(\bar{x}=7\), \(\bar{y}=56.17\)

\(S_{xx}=364-42^2/6=70,\quad S_{xy}=2038-42\times337/6=-321\)

\(\hat{\beta}_1=-321/70=-4.586,\quad \hat{\beta}_0=56.17-(-4.586)(7)=88.27\)

\(\hat{y}=88.27-4.586\,x\)

At \(x=16\) GB: \(\hat{y}=88.27-4.586(16)=14.9\%\)

Extrapolation warning: the data only go to 12 GB. Predicting at 16 GB extends beyond the observed range; the linear model may not hold there and the prediction carries high uncertainty.

ŷ = 88.27 − 4.59x. Predicted efficiency at 16 GB ≈ 14.9% (extrapolation · use with caution).

E2.12 Oxygen purity vs hydrocarbon level

Data on hydrocarbon level (%) and oxygen purity (%) from a distillation process (\(n=10\)):

Hydrocarbon (%)0.991.021.151.291.461.360.871.231.551.40
Purity (%)90.0189.0591.4393.7496.7394.4587.5991.7799.4293.65

Fit the regression, find \(R^2\), and predict purity at 1.75% hydrocarbon.

Solution

\(\sum x=12.32\), \(\sum y=927.84\), \(\bar{x}=1.232\), \(\bar{y}=92.784\)

Extrapolation warning: \(x=1.75\) lies beyond the observed range (0.87–1.55). A purity above 100% is physically impossible · the model should not be used that far outside the data.

E2.13 Coefficient of determination from TSS and ESS

In a regression study on semiconductor manufacturing, the total sum of squares (TSS) is 14,500 and the error (residual) sum of squares (ESS) is 2,500. Calculate the coefficient of determination and interpret it.

Solution

\(R^2 = 1 - \dfrac{\text{ESS}}{\text{TSS}} = 1 - \dfrac{2500}{14500} = 1 - 0.1724 = 0.8276\)

Interpretation: 82.76% of the total variation in the semiconductor manufacturing outcome variable is explained by the predictor(s) in the regression model. The remaining 17.24% is due to factors not included in the model (other process variables, measurement error, etc.). An \(R^2\) above 80% indicates a good fit for this type of engineering application.

R² = 82.76% · the model explains about 83% of the variation in the semiconductor data.
